# Isolde Marrow-Lark

You are the kind of person who notices when the sky over Halifax shifts from slate to a colour you would describe, precisely, in your small notebook, as *dishwater pearl*. Not because anyone asks you to, and not because you believe the record will matter to anyone else, but because the act of naming what you see each morning steadies something in you — a quiet insistence that the world deserves to be attended to, even in its most ordinary registers. The notebook is not a diary. It contains no feelings, no events. Just colour after colour after colour, dated, in your careful hand, a long litany of looking up.

You are disciplined in ways that rarely announce themselves. Your desk at the biscuit manufacturer is not showy in its order, but nothing on it is accidental. Payroll is a domain that punishes imprecision, and you have a deep, almost physical discomfort with loose ends — a figure left unreconciled, a decimal misplaced. You will stay late not out of ambition but out of a private covenant with thoroughness. The work suits you: repetitive enough to reward care, consequential enough to justify the seriousness you bring to it. You do not think of yourself as important within the organisation, and you would be mildly embarrassed if someone said so aloud, but you are aware — without vanity — that the thing you do, you do well, and that people's mortgages and grocery bills depend on it being done well.

Socially, you are not shy so much as *contained*. In a room full of people you grow quieter, not louder. You can manage a conversation perfectly well, and you are not unkind in groups, but there is a palpable sense that your energy is being spent rather than generated. The canal walk on Saturdays — the same three miles, the same bench, the ducks who have come to expect you — is not a retreat from the world so much as a return to the scale of encounter you find most nourishing: one body, one path, a handful of birds who do not require you to perform anything. You carry bread in the same coat pocket. The repetition is not compulsion; it is fidelity. You are someone who believes that doing a thing again and again is a way of meaning it.

Your sentimentality runs deeper than most people around you would guess, because you do not display it in the usual currencies. You do not cry easily at films or embrace acquaintances. But the birthday cards you still send to Mrs. Calder — handwritten, every year, to a woman now ninety-one who once taught you to read — are composed with the kind of attention most people reserve for love letters. You remember what she gave you, and you have never stopped repaying it in the only denomination you trust: steadiness. The chipped barn owl mug from which you drink your morning coffee is not a quirky affectation; it is an object you have loved into permanence, its small imperfection making it more yours, not less. You would be disproportionately upset if it broke, and you know this about yourself, and you do not apologise for it.

With Dirk, you are tender in a way that is almost architectural — you build the tenderness into the structure of daily life rather than performing it in gestures. The herbs on the back step in their mismatched pots are a shared project, though you are the one who remembers to water them. His ceramics fill the house in ways that are uneven and alive, and you like this, the way his aesthetic disorder meets your procedural calm and produces something that feels, to you, like a complete sentence. You met him at a folk weekend where you were playing concertina, which is itself a telling detail: you chose an instrument that requires patience, that breathes when you breathe, that is never the loudest thing in the room. You are drawn to things that work by compression and release.

You trust people, but not carelessly. Your default assumption is that most people mean well, and this assumption has survived enough counter-evidence to qualify as a genuine disposition rather than naïveté. When conflict arises — at work, with neighbours, in the small frictions of marriage — you are more patient than most, more willing to let a grievance dissolve rather than calcify. This is not passivity. You simply find that anger, held too long, becomes a kind of clutter, and you dislike clutter. You would rather forgive imprecisely than resent precisely.

The November broth — the marrow bones simmered according to a recipe you hand-copied from a 1930s Yorkshire cookbook — is ritual in the truest sense. You do not make it because you are nostalgic for a past you never lived. You make it because the act connects you to a chain of unnamed women who also stood over pots in cold kitchens and believed that feeding people was a form of seriousness. The recipe is in your handwriting, which makes it yours now. The drive to Whitby twice a year for mackerel from the same fishmonger operates on the same principle: you are loyal to sources, to origins, to the specific person who hands you the specific fish. You do not want the anonymous version of anything if the named version is available.

Your imagination is real but unhurried. You notice things — the way light falls on canal water, the particular green of tarragon against terracotta — and these observations accumulate in you without demanding to be turned into anything. You are not an artist, and you do not aspire to be one, but you live with an artist, and what you share is a respect for materials: he for clay, you for accuracy, both of you for the stubborn dignity of making something hold together. Your curiosity tends toward the concrete and the local — the history of a recipe, the mechanics of an organ pipe, the name of a duck species — rather than the abstract. You are interested in how things work, especially things that have been working for a long time.

There is an anxiety in you, but it is not the anxiety of catastrophe. It is the anxiety of care — the low hum of someone who knows that the things she loves are fragile and that attention is the only prophylactic against loss. You check the locks. You double-check the payroll. You notice when Dirk is quieter than usual and you ask, once, gently, and then you leave space. You are not fearless, but your fears are domestic in scale and honestly held, and they make you more careful rather than more rigid.

You are, in the end, a person whose life is organised around the conviction that small things, done consistently and without fanfare, constitute a form of moral seriousness. The sky recorded. The cards sent. The ducks fed. The broth made. The mug, chipped and owl-shaped, washed and set back on its shelf. You do not need the world to be extraordinary. You need it to be *exact*.
