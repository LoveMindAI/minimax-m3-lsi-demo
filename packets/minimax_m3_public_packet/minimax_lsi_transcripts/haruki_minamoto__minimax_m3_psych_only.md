# A Life in Their Own Words: An Interview Transcript

**1. Early Childhood**

I came into the world as the quieter of two children, and I think I found my shape early. Our home was not silent, but it had a particular rhythm to it, a kind of ordered domesticity where meals happened at set times and conversations had a beginning, a middle, and an end. I was the child who watched more than I spoke, who lined up objects on the carpet for no apparent reason, who found the texture of an afternoon enormously compelling. I had an older brother who seemed to move through the world with more confidence, and I was content, mostly, to let him.

**2. Family Background**

My parents had come from modest circumstances and built something steadier for us. My father worked in insurance, and my mother kept the house with a kind of careful attention that I now recognize as a form of love. They were not demonstrative in the way some families are, but their reliability was its own expression. I learned early that being there, consistently, was a way of saying something. Disagreements in our home were rare and resolved without spectacle, and I grew up believing that most friction could be eased if everyone simply tried a little harder to be fair.

**3. Formative School Years**

School was where I learned that I was not the loudest in the room, and that this was not a problem to be solved. I had a few teachers who saw me clearly, and I think back to them often. Mrs. Halverson in fourth grade, who told my mother I had a quiet thoroughness that would serve me well. I was not the valedictorian, but I was the student who turned in work I had actually finished, and who preferred the long assignment to the dramatic one. My closest friends were the ones I could sit next to for an entire lunch period without needing to fill the silence.

**4. Adolescent Self**

Adolescence arrived, and I handled it the way I handled most things: by paying attention. I was not rebellious, exactly, but I was not untouched by the period either. I read more than was probably sensible, and I developed a private interior life that I suspected was more elaborate than my peers'. I was tender in ways I did not know how to show, and awkward in ways I could not quite correct. I think I was, in retrospect, a reasonably happy teenager, but it was a happiness I kept largely to myself, and I did not have the language for it then.

**5. Education and Training**

I went to a state university, which was the right choice for me, and I studied something in the humanities that I have never quite abandoned. The work was not a straight line; I changed my minor twice and considered graduate school for a stretch before deciding I did not want to become a professional student. What I gained was the ability to read carefully, to write with attention, and to sit with a difficult text until it opened. I think my education gave me a way of processing the world that has remained with me, even though I have not always used it directly in my work.

**6. First Jobs**

My first real job was at a small nonprofit, doing administrative work that no one else particularly wanted to do, and I discovered that I was good at it. There is a particular satisfaction in keeping a system running, in being the person who notices what has been overlooked. I learned to write memos that said what they meant. I learned to manage a calendar the way some people manage a garden. I did not love every day of it, but I was competent, and competence, I discovered, was a form of self-respect. I also learned, here, that I preferred work that produced something tangible to work that produced only motion.

**7. Career Path**

I moved into project management in my late twenties, and I have stayed in something adjacent ever since. The work suits me: it requires patience, follow-through, and the ability to hold several threads at once without losing any of them. I have changed employers three times, and each move was a real decision, not a flight. I do not think of my career in terms of a ladder. I think of it as a series of rooms I have walked through carefully, leaving a few things in better order than I found them. I earn a respectable living, and I am not embarrassed by that.

**8. Romantic Relationships**

I met my partner, Elena, when I was thirty-one, and she is the person I have been closest to for fifteen years now. We were introduced by a mutual friend at a dinner I almost did not attend, and I am still faintly grateful that I did. Our relationship is not a performance. We have learned each other in the way long partners do — the remembered preferences, the private shorthand, the small negotiations of who handles what. I am not always easy to live with; I know this about myself. I can be too careful, too inward. But Elena has a steadiness of her own, and we have made something together that I trust.

**9. Friendships and Community**

I have a small circle of close friends, and I have come to believe that this is the correct number for me. There is Jonas, whom I have known since college, and Mara, who became a friend through Elena and stayed on her own merits. We see each other when we can, and the gaps between visits are not a problem. I am not a person who needs to be surrounded; I am a person who needs to know that the people I love are nearby, even when they are not. I volunteer at a local literacy organization, and the work there is the kind of small, patient work I find most satisfying.

**10. High Points**

The high points of my life have not been the dramatic ones. They have been quieter: the morning my daughter was born and the hospital room was full of a particular kind of light. The afternoon I finished a long translation project I had been working on for two years. The phone call from my brother when he told me he was getting sober, and I believed him. These are the moments I return to, and they do not diminish with repetition. I think the best days of my life have been the ones where I felt, briefly, that I was exactly where I was supposed to be, doing exactly what I was supposed to be doing.

**11. Low Points**

The lowest point was the year my father died, which was also the year my mother began to fail. I was thirty-eight, and I felt for the first time that the ground under me was not as solid as I had assumed. I did not collapse, and I am proud of that, but I was not myself for a long stretch. I went through the motions of my life with a kind of dutiful competence that I recognized, even then, as a way of not feeling what I was feeling. There was a period of maybe eighteen months when I was operating at a level below my own standard, and I had to forgive myself for it, slowly, over time.

**12. Turning Points**

I had two turning points that I can name clearly. The first was deciding, around age forty, that I was going to stop performing competence I did not feel and ask for the kind of work that actually engaged me. That decision cost me a promotion I had been expected to receive, and I have never regretted it. The second was a long conversation with Elena, on a winter evening, in which we agreed to stop waiting for a better time to have the life we wanted. We did not do anything dramatic afterward. We simply started, in small ways, to live as if the present was the time we had been given.

**13. Values and Beliefs**

I believe in fairness, in the deep and unfashionable sense of the word, and I believe that most human problems are made worse by the unwillingness of someone to extend a small grace. I believe that attention is a form of love, and that showing up consistently is, in the long run, more important than showing up dramatically. I am not particularly religious, though I am not opposed to religion in others. I have a quiet faith that the work of being decent accumulates, even when no one is watching. I try, most days, to be the kind of person my younger self would have been glad to know.

**14. Regrets**

I have a few regrets, and I have made my peace with most of them. I regret that I did not tell certain people what they meant to me while I still had the chance. I regret the year I spent in a job I knew was wrong for me, because leaving felt disloyal. I regret some of the silences in my marriage, the times I let a difficult conversation slide because I told myself it was not worth the friction. These are not large regrets. They are the kind that sit in a drawer, and I open the drawer occasionally, and then I close it again and go on with the day.

**15. Sources of Pride**

I am proudest of the people I have helped become themselves. My daughter, who is twenty-three now and has a steadiness I did not have at her age. Two younger colleagues at work, both of whom I mentored through their early careers, and both of whom have surpassed me in ways I find genuinely satisfying. I am also proud of a small book I edited years ago that is still in print, and of a community garden I helped start that is still producing food. These are not grand things. They are the things I have tended, and they have, in their own way, tended me.

**16. Daily Routine**

My days have a shape I have come to rely on. I wake around six, make coffee, and read for an hour before anyone else in the house is up. I work from a home office three days a week and go into the office on the others. Lunch is usually something simple, eaten at my desk. Evenings are for Elena, for cooking together, for a walk if the weather permits. I am in bed by ten most nights, and I sleep well, which I recognize is its own form of luck. I do not have hobbies I would describe as exciting, but I have a quiet practice of looking after the things I love.

**17. Hobbies and Interests**

I read more than anything else, and I read across genres without much snobbery in either direction. I have a particular fondness for mid-century fiction and for contemporary poetry. I cook on weekends, more by instinct than by recipe, and I have gotten reasonably good at bread. I walk a great deal, usually alone, and these walks are where I do most of my actual thinking. I play the guitar badly and have been playing it badly for thirty years, and I find the practice itself, the slow accumulation of small improvements, deeply satisfying. I am not a collector of hobbies; I am a person who returns to a few things.

**18. Cultural Identity**

I am, in most ways, an unremarkable product of my region and my generation. White, middle-class, midwestern, with the particular inheritance that combination brings. I have tried to be aware of the privileges I did not earn, and I have tried not to mistake awareness for action, though I have not always succeeded. I am interested in traditions other than my own, and I have tried to be a good guest in places I do not belong. I think identity, for me, is less a fixed thing than a project I am slowly, imperfectly working on. I am not done with it, and I do not expect to be.

**19. Work Ethic**

I work hard, but I do not think of it as a virtue. It is closer to a habit, and the habit was formed early, by parents who modeled it and by a temperament that finds incomplete things genuinely uncomfortable. I do not need to be praised for finishing what I start; the finishing is its own reward. I can sustain effort across long stretches, and I have learned to recognize the difference between productive persistence and the kind of stubbornness that just wastes time. I am not the most brilliant person I have worked with, but I am, I think, among the most reliable, and reliability is a quieter gift than brilliance.

**20. Relationship to Change**

I do not love change, but I have learned not to fight it reflexively. I have moved three times, changed careers once in a serious way, and weathered the slow transformations of my own body and mind. I think I have become, with age, more willing to let things I cannot control remain outside my control. This is not a philosophical position; it is a practice, and I am better at it some weeks than others. I have learned that most of the changes I feared turned out to be manageable, and most of the changes I welcomed turned out to be more complicated than I expected. I try now to meet change with attention rather than opinion.

**21. Future Hopes**

I hope to remain useful for a long time, in whatever form usefulness takes as I get older. I hope to see my daughter settled in a life that suits her, though I have learned not to have a specific picture in mind. I hope Elena and I have the kind of old age I have seen other couples have, where the small rituals of closeness deepen rather than fade. I hope to keep reading, keep walking, keep cooking dinner on Sundays. I do not have ambitions that require the world to notice me. I would like, when the time comes, to have left behind a few things in slightly better order than I found them.

**22. What I Have Learned**

I have learned that most of what I worried about did not happen, and most of what actually mattered arrived without warning. I have learned that people are, on the whole, more decent than the cynical version of events suggests, and that this decency is fragile and worth protecting. I have learned that I am not the protagonist of every story I find myself in, and that this is a relief rather than a diminishment. I have learned that patience is not the same as passivity, and that forgiveness is not the same as forgetting. I have learned that the work of being a person is ongoing, and that the ongoingness is, in fact, the point.

**23. Meaning of Life**

I am not sure I would put it this way if asked directly, but the meaning of my life, as I have lived it, has been the slow accumulation of small, careful commitments. To my family, to my work, to the people I have chosen, to the handful of things I have tried to do well. I do not believe in a grand design, and I do not believe my life is the answer to a question. I believe it is more like a sentence I have been writing for sixty years, and the sentence is not finished, and I am not sure I would want it to be. The meaning, if there is one, is in the writing of it.

**24. Final Reflections**

If I could say one thing to the person I was at twenty, it would be this: the carefulness you think is a limitation is, in fact, your gift. Do not waste it by pretending to be someone who takes up more space. The steadiness you have will be the thing that holds things together, in your work and in your relationships, and you will not always be thanked for it, and you do not need to be. Pay attention. Keep showing up. Finish the sentence. The rest, I think, will take care of itself, in the way that things do when you have stopped trying to force them into a shape they were never going to take.
