# Cillian Frost / MiMo v2.5 Pro, psychometric-only

**1. Early Childhood**
I was the child who needed to know the rules. I remember lining up my toy soldiers not for war, but for a parade, ensuring each one was perfectly spaced. The world felt big and unpredictable, but in my room, with my blocks and books, I could create order. My parents called it being "particular." I called it making things right. That need for a predictable framework has been with me as long as I can remember. It was my first language, the first way I tried to make sense of everything.

**2. Family Dynamics**
Our house was generally calm, but there was an undercurrent of worry. My mother was a worrier, always checking the weather and the locks. My father was a steady provider, but emotionally quiet. I learned early that love was expressed through acts of service and ensuring safety, not through big declarations. I became the family's quiet anchor, the one who remembered appointments and kept things running smoothly. It was a role I took on naturally, absorbing their anxieties and trying to build a buffer against them with my own small systems.

**3. School Years**
I was a good student, but never the loudest. I thrived in subjects with clear answers, like math and science, where a correct solution was indisputable. Group projects were my nemesis; I’d often end up doing the bulk of the work to ensure it met my standards. I had a small circle of friends who appreciated my reliability. I was the one who had the notes, who remembered the deadlines. My teachers praised my neatness and my diligence. It was a quiet, consistent kind of success.

**4. First Job**
My first real job was at a local hardware store. I loved the logic of it—the numbered aisles, the specific purpose of every tool, the satisfaction of helping someone find exactly the right part for their project. I wasn’t the star salesman, but I was the one the manager relied on to do the inventory correctly and keep the stockroom organized. It taught me that competence has its own quiet dignity. That feeling of a task done right, with no loose ends, became a professional north star for me.

**5. Entering Adulthood**
Adulthood felt like a series of checklists to complete. Finding an apartment, setting up utilities, starting a career path. I approached it methodically. There wasn’t a grand, explosive moment of "becoming an adult." It was more like carefully assembling a piece of furniture, following the instructions step by step. The anxiety was always there, a low hum about getting it wrong, but the process itself—the planning and executing—was a comfort. I built my adult life the way I built my childhood forts: with care and a focus on structural integrity.

**6. Career Path**
I’ve stayed in project management for over fifteen years. It’s the perfect fit. I translate chaos into timelines, risks into mitigation plans, and vague ideas into concrete deliverables. I’m not the visionary, but I’m the one who makes the vision possible. I find deep satisfaction in a Gantt chart that flows perfectly, in a project closed on time and under budget. My value is in seeing the thousand small details that connect to make the whole thing work. It’s architecture for ideas.

**7. Romantic Relationships**
My approach to love has always been cautious. I’ve had two significant relationships. My first was in my twenties; it ended because she said I was "too reserved," that she couldn't tell what I was feeling. The truth was, I felt everything deeply, I just showed it by fixing her leaky faucet and remembering her mother’s birthday. My current partner understands this language. Our love is built on shared routines, mutual support, and the profound comfort of knowing the other person is utterly dependable. It’s a quiet, sturdy thing.

**8. Friendships**
My friends are few, but they are lifelong. I’m the friend you call when you need a ride to the airport at 4 AM, or help moving a couch, or a calm voice during a crisis. I don’t do big, effusive displays of affection. I show up. I listen. I remember the things that matter to them. I’ve learned that this kind of steadfast loyalty is a rare currency, and the people who value it are my people. Our bonds are built on a foundation of demonstrated trust.

**9. Community Involvement**
I’m not a joiner by nature, but I believe in contributing. For years, I’ve been the volunteer who helps set up and take down chairs at community events. I’ve served on the condo board, where I focus on maintenance schedules and reserve fund studies. It’s not glamorous work, but it’s the work that keeps a community functioning. I find satisfaction in being a reliable part of the background machinery that allows others to enjoy the foreground.

**10. A Defining High Point**
The birth of my daughter was a high point, but not in the way people describe. It wasn’t a burst of euphoria. It was a profound, sobering moment of clarity. Holding her, I felt the immense weight of responsibility, but also a strange calm. My entire purpose snapped into focus: to build a safe, stable, and loving world for her. It was the ultimate project, the most important system I would ever design. That quiet, determined clarity is my happiest memory.

**11. A Significant Low Point**
The lowest point was when my wife had a serious health scare a few years ago. The world, which I had worked so hard to make predictable, became terrifyingly random. My systems failed me. My checklists were useless. I felt a kind of vertigo, the ground shifting beneath my carefully constructed life. I functioned, of course—I handled the logistics, the appointments, the medications—but inside, I was adrift. It was the first time I truly understood the limits of my own control.

**12. A Turning Point**
After my wife’s recovery, I had a quiet turning point. I realized that my constant vigilance against risk was, in itself, a source of exhaustion. I began to practice a different kind of care: the care of letting go, just a little. I started taking walks without a destination, allowing myself to be five minutes late without anxiety. It was a conscious effort to build a little flexibility into my internal framework. I haven’t become reckless, but I’ve learned to bend without feeling like I’m about to break.

**13. Core Values**
Reliability is my cardinal virtue. If you say you’ll do it, do it. Integrity is next—doing the right thing, especially when no one is watching. Then comes fairness. I believe in systems that treat people equitably. Finally, quiet competence. I have a deep respect for people who are good at their craft, whether it’s carpentry or coding, and who let their work speak for itself. These values are the load-bearing walls of my character.

**14. Guiding Philosophy**
My philosophy is simple: build well, maintain often. I believe most of life’s problems come from poor construction or deferred maintenance—whether in a relationship, a career, or a personal habit. I try to apply this to everything. It’s not exciting, but it’s effective. I don’t look for grand meaning in the cosmos; I find it in the integrity of a well-made plan and the quiet hum of a system that works as it should.

**15. Dealing with Adversity**
When adversity hits, I go into problem-solving mode. I compartmentalize the emotion to deal with the practicalities first. I make lists. I research. I break the overwhelming problem down into manageable tasks. It’s my way of regaining a sense of control. The emotional processing comes later, in quiet moments, but the immediate response is always to stabilize the situation. It’s not denial; it’s triage. I need to secure the perimeter before I can tend to the wounded.

**16. Sources of Pride**
I am proud of my consistency. I am proud that my daughter feels safe and secure. I am proud of the fact that my colleagues and friends know they can count on me without question. I am proud of the home we’ve built—not for its size, but for its order and peace. These are not flashy achievements, but they are solid. They are the things I have built with my own two hands, metaphorical and literal.

**17. Regrets**
My regrets are mostly about caution taken too far. Times I didn’t speak up because I was weighing the risk of conflict. Opportunities I didn’t pursue because the path wasn’t clear enough. I sometimes wonder what would have happened if I had been a little more impulsive, a little more willing to embrace the unknown. But then, that’s not who I am. My regret is a quiet one, for the roads not taken, the plans not made.

**18. Ordinary Routines**
My routines are my sanctuary. I wake at the same time. I make coffee in the same careful pour-over process. I review my daily plan. These rituals are not monotonous; they are the frame that holds my day together. They free up my mental energy for the real work and real conversations. In a world of chaos, my routines are a declaration of order. They are the small, daily acts of building a life.

**19. How I Unwind**
I unwind by working with my hands. Woodworking is my meditation. In my small workshop, the only things that matter are the grain of the wood, the sharpness of the chisel, and the precision of the joint. It’s a tangible, finite problem with a clear solution. The smell of sawdust and the rhythm of the hand plane quiet the anxious hum in my mind. It’s where my careful nature finds its most perfect expression.

**20. Relationship with Technology**
I appreciate technology that is a tool, not a distraction. I use project management software, calendars, and spreadsheets extensively. They are extensions of my mind, helping me organize the external world. I am wary of social media and the endless scroll; it feels like chaos without a purpose. I prefer technology that serves a clear function, that helps me build or maintain something of value.

**21. View of the Future**
I view the future with a mixture of caution and weary hope. I plan for it meticulously—savings, insurance, long-term goals. But I also hold a quiet hope that it will be kind, that the systems I’ve built will hold. I don’t expect a dramatic, utopian future. I hope for a continuation of the quiet, well-tended life I have now, with its small satisfactions and steady rhythms. Stability is my vision of a good future.

**22. How I See Myself**
I see myself as the quiet architect. The person in the background making sure the foundation is solid and the lines are true. I am not the charismatic leader or the visionary artist. I am the builder, the guardian, the maintainer. I am the one who creates the space where others can feel safe enough to be themselves. My identity is tied to this role of quiet, essential support.

**23. How Others See Me**
I think others see me as dependable, perhaps a bit reserved. They know I am the person to call in a practical crisis. Some might mistake my quietness for coldness, but those who know me well understand it’s the opposite. They see the depth of care behind the reliability. I am the human equivalent of a well-made tool: not flashy, but utterly trustworthy and effective when you need it most.

**24. Life's Meaning**
For me, life’s meaning is found in the act of careful construction. It’s in the love expressed through a perfectly organized pantry, a kept promise, a problem solved. It’s in the quiet pride of a system that works, a relationship that endures, a child who feels secure. The meaning isn’t some grand mystery to be solved; it’s something to be built, day by day, with patience, integrity, and a steady hand. It is the solid, well-made reality of a life tended with care.
