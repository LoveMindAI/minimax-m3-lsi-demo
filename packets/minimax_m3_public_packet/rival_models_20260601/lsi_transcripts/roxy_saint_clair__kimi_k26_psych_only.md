# Roxy Saint-Clair / Kimi K2.6, psychometric-only

1. Earliest Memory
I remember kneeling on the kitchen floor, arranging wooden blocks into a wall that would stand. My mother cooked behind me, and I felt the warmth of her presence without needing her eyes. When the wall finally held, I didn't call out. I simply sat with it, satisfied in the private way that has never really left me. That feeling of careful construction, of agreeing with myself to do something well—it's still how I recognize myself.

2. Family Climate
My parents moved through our home with quiet purpose, never dramatic, never absent. My father repaired furniture on weekends, sanding by hand what others might have replaced. My mother kept gardens that bloomed in succession, nothing showy, everything timed. They taught me that love could be a patient practice, not a performance. We didn't say much about feelings, but I learned to read the emotional weather—who needed space, who needed proximity. That literacy has served me better than any words.

3. Sibling Shapes
I have one sister, older by four years, who occupied rooms differently than I did. She needed the center; I found the edges comfortable. We weren't rivals so much as adjacent countries with open borders. She'd perform, I'd witness, and sometimes she'd pull me in, surprised by my willingness once there. I think I taught her that intensity wasn't the only form of presence, and she taught me that visibility had its own courage. We're close now in a way that doesn't require constant contact.

4. School Days
School fit me oddly. I wasn't the best student nor the invisible one. I remember the satisfaction of long projects—research papers, science fair constructions—more than any grade. Group work frustrated me when others disappeared, so I often absorbed their portions rather than confront. Teachers sometimes overlooked me for louder talents. I didn't mind, though I see now how that trained a certain useful invisibility. What I loved were the library's quiet floors, the sense of accumulating something slowly.

5. First Loss
My grandmother's death when I was twelve was my first experience of grief as physical weight. I remember sitting in her kitchen after the funeral, touching the worn spots on her chair arms where decades of hands had polished the wood. The permanence of absence opened in me then—not dramatic, but cellular. I began collecting small objects of hers, not for display, but to distribute through my life where I'd encounter them unexpectedly. This is still how I mourn: through placement, through return.

6. Adolescent Currents
Teenage years brought their expected turbulence, but mine moved underground. I had close friends, intense in the way of that age, and I felt everything about them—their romances, failures, shifting alliances—with an embarrassing fullness I learned to hide. I wrote letters I never sent, kept journals in code. What I couldn't express was how much I needed these attachments, how the thought of their ending could hollow an afternoon. I appeared steady. Inside, I was braced against tremors I couldn't name.

7. Finding Work
My first real job was assisting a small architectural restoration firm, documenting old buildings before renovation. The work required patience I didn't know I possessed—measuring, photographing, noting what others would demolish. I found myself speaking for structures that couldn't speak, arguing for preservation with a surprising boldness when the moment required. The owner, a woman in her sixties with mortar permanently under her nails, became my first professional mentor. She saw my quietness not as absence but as attention.

8. Craft and Calling
I've stayed in related work, now managing conservation projects for historic properties. The through-line is care made visible, material. I love the negotiations—what can be saved, what must be replaced, how to honor original intention while serving present need. Each building holds decisions made long ago, adaptations, errors, recoveries. Working with them feels like joining an ongoing conversation across time. My colleagues know me as reliable, thorough, occasionally unexpectedly funny in meetings when I offer something compressed and apt.

9. Romantic Beginnings
I met my partner at a conference on sustainable materials, drawn together during a breakout session that dissolved into practical problem-solving. What I noticed first was her willingness to cede space, to build on others' half-formed ideas rather than supplant them. We talked for hours that evening, then wrote letters—actual letters—for months before geography allowed more. She understood my need for incremental approach, my terror of demanding too much. We've been together fifteen years now, and I still feel surprised by her presence.

10. Domestic Life
Our home is modest, accumulated rather than decorated. We have rituals I treasure without naming them: morning coffee in specific cups, evening walks regardless of weather, the way she reads aloud passages that have moved her. I cook; she cleans. This division emerged without negotiation, simply fit. We don't perform our relationship for others, which sometimes makes it invisible to casual observers. I prefer this. The real weight of a life together lives in textures too fine for display.

11. Friendship Patterns
My friendships are few and enduring, maintained through what others might consider insufficient contact. I remember birthdays imperfectly but appear reliably in crises. There's a small group from various life stages with whom I maintain correspondence—actual letters, sometimes, or long emails that allow the thinking-through I need. I'm drawn to people with their own gravity, who don't require constant entertainment. The test of friendship for me is whether silence together feels comfortable or merely empty.

12. Community Participation
I serve on our neighborhood's historic preservation board, a role that suits my temperament—necessary, unglamorous, requiring patience with process. I've mediated disputes between developers and residents, found compromises that preserved something of value to each side. This work doesn't satisfy my ego; it satisfies my sense of fairness, my belief that most people are trying their best within constraints they didn't choose. The meetings are long. I rarely leave feeling triumphant, often feeling something more durable.

13. High Point
Standing in a restored theater after five years of contentious, incremental work, watching the first performance in decades, I felt something break open in my chest—not pride exactly, but participation. The building had been near demolition; now it held music again. I stood in back, not with the project team in front, and let the sound move through me. My partner found me afterward, crying without embarrassment. "You did this," she said. "We did this," I corrected, meaning everyone, meaning time itself.

14. Low Point
A project I managed failed spectacularly—funding collapsed, political support evaporated, and a building I'd fought for was demolished. I couldn't separate myself from the loss; it inhabited my body for months. I continued functioning, but mechanically, removed from my own experience. What frightened me most was my inability to feel anything beyond numbness. My partner waited, not pressing, present. The return was gradual—a morning when I noticed light on the kitchen table, when something in me reawakened to attachment.

15. Turning Point
In my mid-thirties, a health scare—ultimately resolved—forced confrontation with my own mortality in ways previous losses hadn't. I emerged with clarified priorities: less tolerance for work that felt merely impressive, more commitment to relationships I'd neglected, a sudden urgency about expressing what I felt before the opportunity passed. I began telling people what they meant to me, awkwardly at first, then with something approaching fluency. This hasn't made me dramatic, but it has made me slightly less guarded, more willing to risk the sentiment I'd hidden.

16. Values Clarified
Fairness, for me, is not abstract principle but daily practice—taking only my share of attention, resources, space. I believe in repair over replacement, in the dignity of continuing to care for what exists. I'm suspicious of novelty that discards too easily, of success measured in visibility. What I value most in others is steadiness, the willingness to stay through difficulty without requiring recognition. I want to be someone others can depend on without having to perform gratitude. This seems modest, but it's what I have.

17. Sources of Pride
I'm proud of relationships sustained through difficulty, of projects completed when easier to abandon, of moments when I've spoken up for others who couldn't speak for themselves. I'm proud of my partner's continued choice, renewed daily without guarantee. I'm proud of my hands, their knowledge of materials, their patience. These prides don't announce themselves; they accumulate, sedimentary, available when I need reminding that I've been present in my life, not merely observed it.

18. Regrets
I regret times I absorbed others' failures rather than confront, letting resentment accumulate silently. I regret not telling my father certain things before his death—my admiration, specifically, for his patience with my mother's illness. I regret years spent too guarded, too braced against loss to fully inhabit joy. I regret every moment I chose comfortable invisibility over necessary visibility. These regrets don't paralyze me; they instruct. I move differently now, slightly more open, slightly more willing to be seen.

19. Ordinary Routines
My days have a shape I protect: early rising, coffee in the gray light, correspondence before the world demands response. Work proceeds in focused intervals; I resist the fragmentation of constant availability. Evenings are for reading, sometimes aloud with my partner, sometimes alone. Weekends include long walks where I process what the week didn't allow. These routines aren't rigid; they're the medium in which I think, feel, recover. Without them, I become diffuse, available to everyone, present to no one, including myself.

20. Creative Life
I build small furniture pieces, not professionally but with serious attention—joinery learned from books and failure, wood selected for grain and history. These objects accumulate in our home and with friends. The work connects me to my father, to something pre-industrial, to the satisfaction of fit and finish. I don't display or sell them; I give them to people I love, or simply live with them, letting them age into the life we've built together. This is my art, private, patient, unheralded.

21. Relationship to Nature
I need contact with growing things, with weather, with the patience of seasons. Our small garden is my meditation, not designed for display but for encounter—tomatoes I'll taste, flowers that return without reminder. Walking in woods or along shorelines restores something urban life erodes. I'm not athletic; I'm attentive. I notice what changes, what persists, what returns. This attention feels related to my professional work, to my way of loving—patient, incremental, trusting in processes larger than my will.

22. Current Challenges
Age brings new negotiations with limitation—physical, professional, the narrowing of futures that once seemed infinite. I find myself more aware of time's acceleration, of projects I won't complete, conversations I haven't had. My partner and I are learning new geometries of care as her mobility changes. These aren't tragedies; they're the ordinary difficulty of continuing. What helps is the long practice of incremental attention, of building through faithful repetition what cannot be rushed or forced or finally guaranteed.

23. Future Hopes
I hope to continue, which is itself a hope against the knowledge of ending. I hope for more mornings with coffee and gray light, more walks, more letters to write and receive. I hope to complete certain projects, but more to remain present to their doing. I hope my partner and I continue finding new forms of closeness as old forms become impossible. I hope to be remembered by a few as someone who stayed, who adjusted, who felt with rather than merely alongside. This seems sufficient.

24. Making Meaning
I understand my life as craft, not performance—something built through faithful attention to materials at hand, including my own limitations. Meaning emerges not in grand gestures but in continuity: returning to relationships, to work, to places, despite difficulty, despite the temptation to begin fresh elsewhere. I belong to what I've stayed with. The attachments that have formed me, the buildings I've helped preserve, the small objects I've made and given—these are my biography, written in matter and time. I ask no more than this: to have been present, to have cared carefully, to leave others intact, perhaps even enlarged.
