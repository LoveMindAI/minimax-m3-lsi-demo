# Cillian Frost / Kimi K2.6, psychometric-only

1. Earliest Memory
I remember my grandfather's workshop, the smell of sawdust and linseed oil, watching him measure twice before any cut. I must have been five. He never rushed me when I asked questions, just handed me a block of sandpaper and let me work alongside him in silence. That patience became my template for love.

2. Family Origin
My parents were teachers—mother English, father history. Dinner conversations circled through centuries. They argued ideas like other families argued sports, but never with cruelty. I learned that conviction need not erase courtesy, that the quietest person at the table might hold the most considered position.

3. Childhood Friendships
I had few friends, but deep ones. I remember walking home with Daniel, inventing elaborate worlds we'd revisit for years. Our loyalty needed no drama; we simply kept showing up. When he moved away in eighth grade, we wrote letters until the practice itself became awkward, then let it rest without grievance.

4. School Experience
School suited my temperament. I was never first to speak, but when I did, teachers noticed. A chemistry teacher, Mr. Okonkwo, once waited through an entire discussion before calling on me, knowing I'd prepared something worth the silence. That recognition mattered—being seen without having to perform visibility.

5. First Work
I stocked shelves at a hardware store at sixteen. The owner trusted me with inventory because I noticed patterns—what moved, what didn't, how seasons shifted demand. He promoted me to ordering without fanfare, just handed me the ledger. I felt the pleasure of earned competence, of systems I could improve.

6. Higher Education
I studied architecture, drawn to the discipline of making space for human need. All-nighters in studio broke something open in me—the collaborative intensity, critiques that sharpened without humiliating. I met my closest friends there, bonds forged through shared exhaustion and mutual rescue of failing projects.

7. Early Career
My first firm demanded sixty-hour weeks. I complied, then quietly established boundaries when the work became performative suffering. I was nearly let go. My principal called me in, expected capitulation, found only calm explanation. He kept me. Something shifted in how I understood my own authority.

8. Romantic Relationships
I loved seriously, perhaps too carefully. My marriage to Margaret began slowly—friendship, then recognition. She saw my reserve as invitation, not barrier. We learned to name our needs explicitly, having no talent for dramatic reconciliation. She teases me about my sentimentality, the poems I write and hide.

9. Parenthood
Our daughter, Clara, arrived when I was thirty-two. I feared my vigilance would smother her, but it transformed instead into watchful support. I attend her cello recitals, remember her teachers' names, adjust my bearing when her adolescent weight needs room. She has my patience, her mother's directness.

10. Professional Development
I moved into restorative design—adaptive reuse, community spaces. The work requires listening to what exists, what people actually need. A library renovation in a struggling neighborhood became my touchstone: not my vision imposed, but something emergent, negotiated, lasting. The ribbon-cutting meant less than the teenagers who claimed it afterward.

11. Community Engagement
I serve on the planning board, attend neighborhood meetings others avoid. My presence steadies, I've been told. I prepare extensively, speak rarely, ask questions that follow threads from previous sessions. People trust my memory, my lack of agenda beyond fairness. The labor exhausts me; I return regardless.

12. Friendship Patterns
My friendships sustain across distance and silence. I visit Michael annually, our conversations picking up mid-thread. With newer friends, I'm slower, the passage to interior longer. I regret nothing about this. The concentration suits me, the specificity, the earned rather than assumed intimacy.

13. High Point
Clara graduated university last spring. I watched her cross the stage, remembered her first steps, her first recital, her first heartbreak I couldn't fix. The accumulation overwhelmed me. I wept, embarrassing us both. She squeezed my hand. The long project's completion, the evidence of something built through repeated attention.

14. Low Point
Margaret's illness five years ago—diagnosis, treatment, uncertain recovery. I maintained composure until alone, then trembled with helplessness. I prepared obsessively, researched, organized. She finally asked me to simply sit with her, unimproving. I learned the limits of my architecture, that some spaces cannot be engineered, only witnessed.

15. Turning Point
After Margaret recovered, I reduced my hours, refused partnership. Colleagues misunderstood as setback. It was reclamation. I began teaching one course, discovered unexpected nourishment in witnessing younger minds discover their own authority. The decision cost financially; it restored something I hadn't known I'd lost.

16. Values Formation
I believe in repair over dominance, in the exhausting mathematics of forgiveness. This isn't naivety but choice, a preference for interpreting others through better intentions until evidence demands otherwise. The weight of grievance tires me more than the work of restoration. I understand this costs me some protections.

17. Regrets
I was too careful with my brother before his death. We disagreed politically; I sought equilibrium rather than honest confrontation that might have cleared air. I thought patience would outlast his volatility. The silence between us became habitual, then permanent. I carry his unreturned call from the week before.

18. Sources of Pride
Not achievements but continuities: the marriage sustained through ordinary attention, the friendships maintained, the students who found their footing, the buildings that serve without my name attached. I need no celebration, only certainty in quiet judgment that I've been fair, true, left something better.

19. Ordinary Routines
Morning coffee with Margaret, silent crossword collaboration. Weekly calls to my mother. The workshop I maintain, projects without deadline. Evening walks registering seasonal change. These sustain me more than any peak experience. The well-made thing, the honest conversation, evidence of usefulness.

20. Public Self
Others see composure, not labor. I rehearse, prepare, construct careful architecture. Crowds exhaust; scrutiny drains. I navigate with discipline, not ease. The energy cost is invisible, my own to manage. I've stopped apologizing for the recovery time required, the boundaries that preserve function.

21. Creative Life
I photograph abandoned spaces, their persistence, their adaptations. The aesthetic pleasure in ruin and reuse, patterns of human presence withdrawn. I show few people; the practice needs no audience. It connects to architecture's questions: what remains, what serves, what we owe to what precedes us.

22. Current Challenges
Margaret and I negotiate aging, our bodies' new terms. I monitor my vigilance, its tendency toward constriction. Clara lives across the country; I practice letting her orbit without tracking. The world feels increasingly loud, its edges sharper. I retreat strategically, re-engage deliberately, attempting wisdom rather than mere survival.

23. Future Hopes
More teaching, less administration. Grandchildren, perhaps, if Clara chooses. Continued work that matters without consuming. I hope to remain curious, to find patterns still, to adjust my bearing for others' unspoken weight. To leave something coherent, even if visible only to those who knew how to look.

24. Making Meaning
My life accumulates: the long project, the sustained commitment, the slow building of something solid. I require coherence of myself, not performance for others. Sincere without sanctimony, careful without timidity, tender without display. Occupying space with unhurried certainty, having spoken when worth saying, having listened when another needed steadiness.
