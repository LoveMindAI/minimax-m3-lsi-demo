# Roxy Saint-Clair / MiniMax M2.7, interwoven biography

# A Life in Sections

## 1. The Bakery Beneath Us

Our apartment sat on the second floor, right above my grandmother's bakery on Government Street. I'd wake to the percussion of flour sacks being hauled, the clatter of sheet pans, and that yeasty warmth seeping through the floorboards into my bedroom. I was always slow to rise because the ceiling of my consciousness seemed made of bread. My mother worked the early shift downstairs, and on winter mornings she'd send my father up with a warm tortilla wrapped around butter and sugar. I can still taste that sweetness. I think it shaped me before I had words for what sweetness meant.

## 2. Blood and Belonging

My mother was a quiet river, always moving, always present. My father taught me patience through fishing expeditions where hours passed without a bite and he never complained. But the person who shaped me most was Uncle Raymond, my mother's brother, who lived in Gonzales on a property with oak trees so old they seemed to be waiting for something. He was never married, never had children of his own, so when I came along he became something between a second father and a devoted audience for every story I brought him. He taught me that love could be a forty-minute drive every Sunday, a commitment made in repetition.

## 3. The Classroom and Its Quiet

I was the child who noticed when the janitor looked tired, when a classmate's shoes were too large. Teachers liked me because I turned work in on time, but I think they sensed something else in me—a watcher, a listener. School was never about the grades for me; it was about the room itself, the way light fell through the windows onto desks, the particular quality of silence right before a fire drill. I learned early to read a room, to feel its moods. This made me good at some things, lonely at others. I had friends but I kept them at a distance, as if I was already practicing for a loneliness I'd been warned about but never experienced.

## 4. The Magnolias in Spring

The parish was always exploding with something. Azaleas in March so thick they blotted out the facades of houses. Then the magnolias, waxy and white, lasting longer if you knew when to cut them. At seventeen I sat in the chair of a woman named Marlene who worked out of a converted gas station, and I asked for a magnolia on my left forearm. My mother wasn't angry, just quiet for a long time, which was worse. But some marks are not about rebellion; they're about claiming something you want to carry forever. Mine has faded now, the edges softened by twenty years of sun and soap and skin, but I can still trace its ghost when I wash my hands.

## 5. The Weight of a Storm

I've always felt storms coming in my chest before they arrived in the sky. There's a pressure, a thickness in the air that sets my pulse quickening. As a child I would go to the window and watch the light change, greenish and strange, feeling simultaneously terrified and alive. I still keep weather apps open on my phone, checking obsessively as systems form in the Gulf. I think it's more than superstition or sensitivity. I think it's a kind of empathy for the sky itself, for the way Louisiana can turn on you in an hour. My uncle once told me that people like us feel the world before it happens. I think that's why I'm drawn to real estate—helping people find shelter from whatever's coming.

## 6. The Night's Quiet Education

At nineteen I became a night dispatcher for emergency services, working the midnight to eight shift in a room lit by the glow of six monitors. The radio crackled with voices freighted with fear, and I learned to parse the difference between panic and exhaustion, between the caller who needed police and the one who needed someone just to listen. Those years taught me that most people, when they're in crisis, are not looking for solutions. They're looking for witness. I learned to keep my voice steady even when my hands shook, to say the right thing even when there was no right thing. The night taught me about the elasticity of courage, and I've carried that lesson into every transaction, every difficult conversation since.

## 7. The Uncle in Gonzales

Every Sunday I drive to Gonzales. The road unspools in the same direction each time, and I've come to love the familiarity of it, the way the highway markers become a rosary I'm counting. My uncle Raymond is seventy-three now, and his solitude has deepened into something I can't fully name. He sits on his porch with his hands folded, and when I arrive he doesn't stand, just turns his head and nods. I cook red beans and rice because it's what his sister, my mother, used to make, and I want the taste to persist even though she's been gone seven years. We eat in almost complete silence, and the silence is not empty. It holds everything we can't say. I leave before dark so he can settle into his evening alone, and I always wonder if my visits are enough, if presence is still a form of love even when words have abandoned the building.

## 8. The Shape of the Sedan

My silver sedan is fourteen years old, and I've never wanted another car. The driver's seat has molded itself to my particular slump, and I can't imagine another vehicle knowing my shoulders as well. There's a dent in the rear bumper from the time I backed into a mailbox in a rainstorm, and I've left it untouched. It reminds me that I'm not interested in perfection, that scars can be honest. Every first of the month I swap out the pine-tree air freshener, and the ritual has become a kind of meditation. Clean scent, new month. The car smells like bakery flour and warm asphalt, a combination that is probably imaginary but feels true to the accumulation of my life. I drive a lot, and the car is not just transportation. It's a room I inhabit, a container for all the miles between the people I tend and the work that fills me.

## 9. The Tender Heart

My therapist once called me "a high-empathy individual," and the phrase stuck because it felt clinical enough to be manageable. I feel the world in ways that exhaust me. When a buyer hesitates over a cracked tile, I feel their fear of being foolish, of failing their family, of making a decision that will follow them. When I see a stray cat on my porch, I feel its distrust as my own shame. My uncle's sighs reach me across the silence of our Sunday meals, and I want to fix whatever is causing them but know I can't. This tenderness is not always a gift. Sometimes it's a weight that makes ordinary days feel like they're pressing on my chest. But I've learned to see it as a compass rather than a curse. It points me toward the places where care is needed, and I try to answer that call even when it costs me.

## 10. The Clarinet's Breath

At eleven I received a clarinet from my father, who had found it at a pawn shop on Plank Road. He didn't know how to play, had no idea if I would, but something in his face when he handed it over suggested hope. I learned slowly, frustratingly, at first hating the shrieks that emerged when I pressed wrong combinations of keys. But somewhere in high school, after my mother's death, the clarinet became the only place where my grief made sense. I would play late at night, working through jazz standards, finding in the melodies a way to say what I couldn't speak. The clarinet requires breath, requires life itself passing through the instrument, and I think that's why I love it. It keeps me honest about the fact that I am alive, that air moves through me, that music is just organized air and I'm the one organizing it.

## 11. Discipline and Detail

My notebook of inspection details is a record of care taken seriously. Every crack noted in neat script, every repair prioritized by urgency and cost. I've built my reputation as a real estate agent on thoroughness: I return calls the same day, I show up to appointments early, I treat each transaction as a promise. This discipline comes from my years as a night dispatcher, when the difference between doing something right and doing it wrong could be measured in minutes. But it also comes from something deeper—a belief that the small things matter, that how you handle the details is who you are when no one is watching. I find satisfaction in the smooth choreography of a successful closing, in the way chaos becomes order through patience and persistence.

## 12. Reading Rooms

I can walk into a house and feel its history. Not literally, not ghostlike, but in the way the light falls, the way the walls are arranged, the particular wear patterns on the floors. I've learned to read a buyer's unspoken needs as carefully as I read the rooms themselves. When someone hesitates, I don't push; I wait. When someone's eyes linger on a particular corner, I note it. This skill, if it is a skill, came from listening to the radio all those nights, learning to hear what was underneath the words. Now I apply it to architecture and human desire, to the way people want to live and the stories they tell themselves about shelter. A house is never just a house. It's a container for a life, and I try to help people find the container that fits.

## 13. Community's Uncertain Edge

At neighborhood meetings I'm both bold and uneasy. I raise my hand, my voice steady, my arguments clear, but inside my chest there's always a flutter, a awareness that I'm being measured. Still, I show up. I believe that community is built from showing up, from speaking even when your voice trembles, from offering flexibility when others dig in. I've mediated disputes between neighbors over fence lines and drainage, and I've learned that most conflicts are not about the thing they're supposedly about. They're about feeling heard, about needing someone to acknowledge that the small wounds of daily life are still wounds. A calm word, I've found, can redirect a heated argument like a stone diverting a stream. The water finds a new path, and everyone moves forward, and the damage is less than it might have been.

## 14. The Garden's Yield

Behind my duplex there's a modest vegetable garden that takes up more of my summer than it probably should. I grow tomatoes mainly, the kind that split when overripe, that stain your hands red and smell like warm earth. In August I can them with a careful hand, sealing the season's bounty in glass jars that line my pantry shelf like amber Preservances of August light. My mother canned too, though she did it out of necessity rather than choice, and I think I inherited her relationship to food as both nourishment and preservation. The garden is not about saving money or eating organic, though those are side benefits. It's about tending to something living, about patience and attention, about the satisfaction of eating what you've grown. And it's about my uncle, too—because sometimes I bring him a jar, and he accepts it with a nod that means more than any words.

## 15. The Strays We Keep

There was a cat that showed up at my porch two winters ago, thin and suspicious, with a notched ear that suggested a life of difficult freedom. I put out food, didn't expect anything. Week after week, she stayed, and now she lives in my carport, tolerating my presence with a dignity I admire. I named her Louise, after the bayou that runs past my uncle's property, and I think she knows she's been claimed, though she'd never admit it. I don't need her to be affectionate. I just need her to be here, to accept the bowl of food each morning, to remind me that sometimes love is about showing up with sustenance and not asking for anything in return. She's taught me something about patience that I couldn't have learned from any human relationship.

## 16. High Points in the Low Hours

There was a night when I was twenty-four, deep in my dispatcher years, when a call came in about a lost child in the Atchafalaya Basin. The caller was the father, and his voice was so broken it sounded like something torn. I stayed on the line for two hours, keeping him talking, keeping him walking through the cypress trees with a flashlight, until the sheriff's department found them both. When I hung up the phone, my hands were shaking so badly I couldn't type my incident report. But that night, for the first time, I understood that my presence could be a kind of life raft, that my steady voice could keep someone from drowning. That moment stays with me, rises up in me when I think I'm not doing enough, when I wonder if my small acts of care add up to anything real.

## 17. The Losses That Soften

My mother's death at forty-nine from a stroke that came without warning—that was a hinge moment, before and after. I was twenty-two, just starting the dispatcher job, and suddenly the ceiling that had always been made of bread was just a ceiling. I leaned into work, into the night shifts where exhaustion felt like purpose. I leaned into the clarinet, into melodies that let me grieve without speaking. And I leaned into my uncle, who had lost his sister and never said much about it but let me sit with him in silence. I think grief is something that doesn't end but changes texture over time, becoming less a wound and more a presence you learn to live alongside. The magnolia tattoo was done six months after she died, and in some way it was a claiming of beauty in the midst of absence, a way of saying that life still had room for something I chose.

## 18. Principles in Practice

I believe in fairness the way some people believe in God. It's the organizing principle of my days, the lens through which I read every situation. When I'm working with buyers and sellers, I try to make sure no one feels taken advantage of, that the transaction serves everyone's needs as much as possible. This has probably cost me money—I've walked away from deals where I could have pushed harder, squeezed more from a desperate seller. But I can't sleep at night knowing I've been unfair, knowing I've used someone's vulnerability against them. My father taught me this, not through lectures but through example. He was a man who gave back change when a cashier made an error in his favor, who always assumed the best about the people he dealt with. I inherited that tendency, and it's both a gift and a vulnerability, a way of being in the world that leaves me open to disappointment but also open to trust.

## 19. Regrets in the Rearview

I regret not visiting my mother more when she was in the hospital. Not because I didn't love her but because I was young and stupid and thought there would be more time. She died on a Tuesday, and I had been planning to go that weekend. That gap, that small distance between intention and action, has never closed. I carry it like the dent in my bumper, something left untouched because it reminds me of what I failed to do. And I regret, sometimes, the distance I've kept from certain friendships, the way I've prioritized work and family obligations over the cultivation of peers. I'm not lonely, exactly, but I notice that my life is smaller in some directions than it might have been. I wonder if I traded breadth for depth, and whether that was the right trade.

## 20. Sources of Pride

My uncle is still here. That's the thing I'm proudest of, that I've kept showing up for him every Sunday for years, that despite my own grief and the slow erosion of time, I've maintained that ritual of presence. I'm proud of the way I handled the night dispatcher years, that I emerged from them still capable of tenderness rather than hardened by what I heard. I'm proud of my garden, of the jars on my pantry shelf, of the fact that I can feed myself and others from what I've grown. And I'm proud, quietly, of the clarinet, of the way I've stuck with it for twenty-six years even when progress was slow, even when no one was listening. Pride feels immodest to say aloud, but I think it's just another word for satisfaction in a job well done, and I've tried to do a lot of those.

## 21. Ordinary Routines

A typical day begins at six, the alarm pulling me from sleep into the gray half-light of early morning. I check the weather, always, a reflex that has become as automatic as breathing. Coffee, black, in the same blue mug I've had for fifteen years. I feed Louise before I leave, and she watches me with an expression that might be gratitude or might be calculation; with her it's hard to tell. Then the drive to work, the pine scent of the air freshener mixing with the memory of bakery flour, and I arrive at the office already rehearsing the day's appointments in my head. I make calls, write up listings, meet with clients. I come home to the garden if it's growing season, or to the clarinet if the evening is quiet. The routines are not boring to me; they're the architecture of my days, the skeleton that lets the flesh of spontaneity move and breathe.

## 22. The Aesthetic Sense

I notice doors. Front doors especially, their colors and materials, the way they open or don't, the threshold they represent. I've walked through hundreds of houses and I can tell you which ones have good bones, which ones have been loved, which ones are hiding damage behind fresh paint. But I'm drawn to beauty not just in architecture but in music, in the way a jazz phrase can twist and turn like a bayou, finding its way by what feels like instinct but is actually technique. The clarinet taught me that appreciation and execution are different skills, that you can love something deeply without being its master. I think I've made peace with being a good amateur, someone who plays for the joy of the form rather than the applause of an audience. The music I make late at night is mine alone, and that's what makes it valuable.

## 23. Future Hopes

I hope my uncle has many more Sundays in him, that the drive to Gonzales remains possible for years to come. I hope to expand the garden, to add new varieties, to keep learning the slow lessons of cultivation. I hope to play the clarinet better than I do, to finally master the difficult passages in Coltrane's "Equinox," to feel the music move through me with less effort and more flow. I hope to sell houses that become homes for people who need them, to be the agent who makes a difficult transition a little easier. And I hope, vaguely, for companionship—not urgency, just openness, the sense that the door might open someday onto someone I haven't yet met. I'm not looking, exactly, but I'm not closed either. Hope is a form of patience, and I've learned patience the hard way.

## 24. The Meaning I've Made

Meaning, for me, is not a destination but a daily practice. It's in the jar of canned tomatoes on the shelf, in the clarinet's breath, in the forty minutes of highway that connect me to my uncle. It's in the steady voice I try to keep on the phone with anxious buyers, in the notebook where I record what matters, in the dent I refuse to fix because it reminds me that I'm allowed to be imperfect. I've tried to build a life that is coherent, where the internal landscape matches the external world, where the values I hold are enacted in small, ordinary ways. I don't know if I've succeeded. But I know I'm still trying, still tending, still showing up. And maybe that's enough. Maybe the trying is the meaning, the effort itself the point. I drive my silver sedan through the days, air freshener fresh, dust never quite settled, and I keep going because going is what living looks like from inside this particular body, this particular life.
