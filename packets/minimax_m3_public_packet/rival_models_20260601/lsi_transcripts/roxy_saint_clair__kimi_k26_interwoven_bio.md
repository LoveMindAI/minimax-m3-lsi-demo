# Roxy Saint-Clair / Kimi K2.6, interwoven biography

1. **Early Parish Roots**
I was born in the same shotgun house my grandmother died in thirty years later. The parish raised me collectively—Mrs. Fontenot next door fed me when Mama worked doubles, and Mr. Dupree taught me to patch bicycle tires in his garage. I learned every oak tree's personality, every sidewalk's heave from live oak roots. By eight I could navigate home blindfolded just from the temperature shift between sun and shadow on each block.

2. **Family Geometry**
Daddy left when I was four; I remember only his boots by the door. Mama raised me and my sister with the stubbornness of a woman who refuses to ask twice. She cleaned houses in the Garden District, came home smelling of lemon oil and other people's prosperity. We were poor but never told so, which made all the difference. My sister teaches third grade now, three blocks from where we fought over cereal.

3. **The Sedan and Its Philosophy**
I bought this silver Camry in 2009, the year after Katrina, when reliable transportation meant survival. The dent happened in 2016, a fender-bender on Magazine Street that wasn't my fault but wasn't worth fighting over either. I keep it like I keep faded tattoos—evidence that perfection is a marketing scheme. The pine-tree freshener ritual started accidentally, became sacrament. Some mornings I sit in that evergreen smell before turning the key.

4. **School as Social Geography**
I was never brilliant, always present. Teachers remembered me for attendance, not achievement. High school taught me negotiation—lunch trades, hall passes, group project diplomacy. I graduated seventeenth in a class of ninety-four, the exact middle of respectable. What I learned was how to read a room, who needed saving and who needed space. Those skills proved more durable than algebra.

5. **The Tow Truck Years**
Nineteen to twenty-six, midnight dispatcher for Crescent City Recovery. I talked people through panic, sent help to strangers broken down on the Causeway, learned to locate callers by the last mile marker they remembered. The job wore grooves in my voice—still there, people say, this calm that sounds like I've already seen the worst version of tonight. I left when the schedule started stealing my dreams.

6. **Transition to Houses**
My uncle needed showing help; I needed daylight hours. Turned out I understood what people couldn't articulate about space—who needed a kitchen to host, who needed a bedroom far from streetlight. I got licensed, joined a small agency, built clientele through patience rather than aggression. Now I sell where I grew up because I know which blocks flood first, which corners turn dangerous after dark, which porches catch morning coffee light.

7. **The Magnolia's Persistence**
The tattoo was twenty-two, drunk on cheap wine and the conviction that beauty must be permanent. The artist was learning; the green bled, the petals blurred into something botanical but inaccurate. I've considered cover-ups, sat in three different shops listening to proposals. Each time I walked out. The magnolia failed but tried. I owe it the same loyalty I extend to friends who chose badly once.

8. **Romantic Architecture**
I've loved twice seriously. First, Marcus, who sold insurance and wanted to move to Atlanta; we ended cleanly, still exchange Christmas cards. Second, Denise, who restored furniture and understood my silences; she died in 2019, sudden, unfair, a blood clot traveling like a secret. Since then, I've dated carefully, ended things before they became obligations. I'm not closed, just calibrated differently now.

9. **Conflict as Craft**
My sister calls it my superpower, this ability to find the overlapping need. I don't enjoy winning at others' expense—the victory tastes metallic, temporary. In real estate, this means explaining why a lower offer from a local teacher matters more than cash from a developer. I've lost commissions to my own principles. The commission I keep, I sleep beside peacefully.

10. **The Paperwork Devotion**
Contracts, disclosures, inspection reports—I alphabetize, color-code, maintain files years past requirement. This discipline came from dispatching, where a wrong address meant someone stranded longer in danger. Now it means my clients never scramble for documents, never face surprises at closing. The organization is love made visible, my substitute for flowers or dramatic speeches.

11. **Community Maintenance**
I know which elderly homeowners need gutters checked, which young families struggle with property tax appeals. I bring casseroles to closings, attend funerals of clients' parents, remember children's names across decades. The parish isn't geography anymore; it's this accumulated attention. People call me for recommendations—plumbers, dentists, divorce attorneys—because I've become a switchboard of local trust.

12. **High Point: The Robinson House**
2017, I helped the Robinsons buy the house where Mrs. Robinson's grandmother had worked as a maid. The current owners resisted; I found the overlap—preservation tax credits, a delayed closing that let them find comparable space. At closing, three generations cried. I made less than usual. That check remains uncashed in my memory, more valuable than any commission.

13. **Low Point: The Flood Season**
2016, I showed a house I knew had flooded, failed to emphasize it properly. The buyers discovered mold six months in. They didn't sue—our overlap again, their mercy—but I knew. I paid for remediation anonymously, left the profession for three months, drove my dented sedan to Texas and back without purpose. The guilt became the dent's twin, visible only to me.

14. **Turning Point: Denise's Death**
Her furniture restoration shop sits empty still; I pay the taxes, can't yet let it go. Her death taught me that continuity breaks, that my preference for mutual benefit assumes time exists for reciprocity. I returned to work too soon, made mistakes, then found a rhythm where grief didn't disable but deepened. I listen differently now. I hear urgency beneath politeness.

15. **Values: The Settled Useful**
New things make me suspicious; they haven't proven themselves. I prefer the knife that fits my hand after years, the recipe adjusted through failure, the friendship that requires no performance. This isn't nostalgia but empiricism—I trust what has survived. In houses, I steer clients toward solid bones over fresh paint. The cosmetic is deception; the structural is promise.

16. **Regret: The Unfinished Conversation**
Marcus, before Atlanta, asked me to come. I said maybe, meant no, lacked the courage for clarity. We could have ended cleaner, remained closer. Instead, years of polite distance, the waste of a genuine connection. I think of this when clients hesitate, when I see them calculating sacrifice against desire. I push harder now for honest speech, even when it costs.

17. **Sources of Pride**
The Robinson house. Three former clients who became foster parents because I found them space with an extra room. My sister's children, who call me for advice they don't take their parents'. The tow truck drivers who still text me storm warnings. These aren't achievements but accumulations, the residue of showing up consistently. Pride feels like gratitude in different lighting.

18. **Ordinary Routines**
Morning: coffee at home, the same mug from 2004, no sugar. Midday: drive the parish slowly, noting new paint, missing shingles, which for-sale signs have aged. Afternoon: paperwork, calls, the negotiation of small arrangements. Evening: dinner often alone, sometimes with my sister's family, occasionally a date that ends early and kindly. Night: read until the words blur, sleep without alarm.

19. **The Parish as Body**
I feel it physically now—anxiety in my shoulders when a historic house sells to developers, ease when a young couple moves into a block I watched decline. My posture carries this, the weight of watching, the responsibility of witness. I'm not the parish's savior, just its chronicler, its matchmaker between people and the light they'll wake to. This is enough. This is the work.

20. **Future Hopes: The Continuation**
I want to train someone, pass the accumulated knowledge—which corners, which light, which histories. I want to see the Robinson grandchildren in that house. I want to forgive the magnolia or finally cover it, one or the other, no more hesitation. I want to love again without the calibration of loss, though I suspect that's impossible and perhaps unwise.

21. **Meaning: The Negotiation Extended**
I used to think meaning was discovery, something found. Now I believe it's construction, built daily from small agreements—this price, that closing date, the promise to call when the inspection completes. Each successful negotiation is a temporary utopia, proof that divergent interests can align. The parish continues because enough people choose mutual benefit over maximum extraction.

22. **The Crisis Voice**
People remark on my calm during difficulty—failed inspections, financing collapses, the small emergencies of property transfer. I hear it sometimes, surprised, this voice from the dispatch years, talking strangers through darkness. It isn't absence of feeling but precedence of function: first stabilize, then process. The processing happens later, alone, in the evergreen smell of the sedan, driving nowhere particular.

23. **What I Refuse**
I refuse cosmetic vanity for properties and people. I refuse the commission that costs someone their stability. I refuse to fortify against forgiveness, though experience has offered every reason. I refuse to repair the dent or bury the magnolia, maintaining these small visible loyalties. I refuse to leave the parish, though Atlanta and other places have made offers of escape.

24. **Final Accounting**
If asked what I've built: not wealth, not reputation beyond local memory, but continuity. Houses passed to hands that understand them. Relationships that survived disagreement. A voice that calms. A sedan that smells of artificial evergreen and genuine intention. The parish in my posture, its streets in my muscle memory, its future partially shaped by my daily choices toward overlap, toward mutual benefit, toward the light.
