# Haruki Minamoto / MiniMax M2.7, interwoven biography

# Life Story Interview

**1. The View from Above**

I grew up in a narrow two-story house perched on a slope above Lake Biwa. From my window, water met earth in a line that never stayed still—always shifting with light, with weather, with the slow seasons. My father never explained landscape to me. He didn't have to. That view taught me everything: that water and earth are not opposites, that horizons are invitations, that slopes hold possibilities. I spent my childhood watching that lake, learning to read the rhythm of a horizon the way a poet reads a line of verse.

**2. The House and My Parents**

My parents were not elaborate people. My mother taught elementary school and kept a small herb garden by the kitchen entrance. My father worked at a printing company but spent his evenings hand-copying poetry into worn notebooks. The house was narrow—we learned to navigate each other's paths without collision. There was a wooden fence that creaked in November wind, a persimmon tree whose bark smelled like earth after rain. They gave me quiet attention, the kind that lets a child grow toward light without being pushed.

**3. Learning to See**

School was unremarkable in the ways people measure. I was not the quickest with numbers, not the loudest in discussions. But I remember walks home through a drainage canal where willows leaned over standing water, and I would stop to watch how light fractured through leaves. My teachers called me "thoughtful," which I later understood meant I was slow to answer. What I was actually doing was listening—not just to words, but to the silence between them. That habit of attention became the foundation of everything that followed.

**4. The Decision to Tend**

I did not arrive at landscape design through a single moment of revelation. It was more like soil settling—gradual, inevitable. After finishing a degree in agricultural science, I found myself drawn to how spaces could hold meaning, not just function. A professor mentioned that gardens are the only art form you can walk inside. That idea lodged in me. I began apprenticing at a local firm, learning to hold a client's vision the way my father held his pen—steady, modest, never pressing too hard.

**5. Meeting Sae**

I met Sae through mutual friends at a small exhibition of ceramic work. She had a way of standing in a room that made others feel less crowded, less pressed. She is a potter—her hands know clay the way mine know soil. We did not fall into conversation immediately; we simply found ourselves walking near each other, then sitting near each other, then not needing to fill silence with words. She steadies me without trying to. When my mind races ahead to deadlines and possibilities, her presence reminds me that clay sets at its own pace.

**6. The Arrival of Koharu**

Koharu came into our lives when I was thirty-four. I remember the weight of her in my arms—how small and certain she seemed, as if she had always been meant to arrive. She changed the texture of my attention. Suddenly I noticed the height of garden paths differently, the reach of a low branch. Her first word was not "mother" or "father" but "leaf"—pointing at the maple outside our window. She sees the world with an uncluttered directness that still humbles me. She insists on adding her own line to my planting sketches. I have learned to let her.

**7. The First Garden**

My first solo commission was a small courtyard for a retired schoolteacher in Kyoto. She wanted something "that feels like memory," she said, which at the time seemed impossible to translate into stone and soil. I walked the site at different hours, watching how morning light fell across the concrete, how rain gathered in certain corners. I designed a narrow channel of water that caught afternoon sun and threw it back against a white wall. When she saw it finished, she touched the wall and did not speak for a long time. That silence was worth more than any praise.

**8. Fairness as Foundation**

I hold sincerity the way a calligrapher holds a brush—steady, modest, never pressing an advantage. When I negotiate a planting scheme with a client, I weigh their wishes against what the soil itself will allow. I will not tell someone that a garden will thrive where it cannot. I have lost commissions because of this honesty. I do not regret it. A garden built on false promises collapses eventually; a garden built on honest collaboration endures. My integrity is not a marketing strategy. It is simply the only way I know how to work.

**9. A Project That Still Grows**

There was a project seven years ago—a rooftop garden for a children's clinic in Osaka. The architect wanted wild, the budget was tight, and I had never worked at elevation before. I spent nights sketching water channels that could double as drainage, planting schemes that would survive wind and shallow soil. The garden is still there. I received a photograph last spring showing a child tending a small plot beside her mother. That image lives in my notebook beside a pressed sprig of thyme from that first planting. This is not sentimentality. This is evidence that the work matters.

**10. When the Pressure Rises**

I will not pretend I am unshakeable. Press a deadline, a public presentation, a room full of strangers—and I feel the hum of anxiety rise in my chest. I find myself quietly rehearsing exits, imagining the words I might say if I could simply leave. I rarely need them. The moment passes, or the work carries me through it. But that tenderness toward stakes is part of me. I feel the weight of things—the fading leaf, the shifting light, the unspoken worry of a neighbor. This sensitivity is not weakness. It is what makes me careful.

**11. A Pivotal Season**

I nearly left the work once. Seven years in, I was exhausted by the gap between what clients imagined and what the land allowed. I spent a winter restoring fountain pens—old Watermans, broken Pelikans—finding comfort in the precise work of repair. Sae watched me without comment. Then one morning she said, "You restore things. Why would you abandon something you've only just begun to understand?" The question was not a challenge. It was an observation. I returned to spring with a different posture—not seeking to control the garden, but to collaborate with it.

**12. What I Believe**

I believe that care is a form of resistance against a world that rushes toward exploitation. I believe that listening—truly listening—is an act of generosity. I believe that a garden is never finished because growth is never finished, and that this impermanence is not a failure but an invitation to return with fresh attention. I believe the soil has its own counsel, and that humility before the natural world is not surrender but wisdom. These are not abstractions to me. They are practices, daily and seasonal, woven into how I move through the world.

**13. What I Did Not Say**

My father died three years before I published my first design manual. He never saw it—the pages on soil pH and light exposure, the photographs of completed gardens. I sent a copy to my mother's house and found it untouched on the shelf months later. She meant no harm by this; grief makes ordinary things invisible. But I carry the regret of not saying clearly enough, while he lived, that his quiet example had shaped me. I write now as if he might read. This is how I answer what I did not say.

**14. The Restored Pens**

On my office shelf, rows of vintage fountain pens stand ready—bought broken, restored with patient hands over winter evenings. A Waterman 52, a Pelikan 400, a Pilot with a cracked barrel I mended with two-part epoxy. The ritual of restoration mirrors how I approach a garden: assess the damage, repair what can be repaired, nurture what remains, and let the natural order reveal itself. Ink flows through these pens now. They write soil notes, correspondence, the first lines of planting plans. Each one is a small triumph of patience over discard.

**15. A Day in Rhythm**

My days follow a quiet rhythm. Morning begins with coffee on the balcony, watching light move across the miniature maple grove I planted in a long container. I check the moisture in three propagation trays before dressing. At the office, I review the day's correspondence with the same attention I give to soil samples—looking for what is unsaid, what might need gentle questions. Evening brings dinner with Sae and Koharu, her crayon scribbles beside my planting sketches. Before sleep, I write a single line in my notebook: one observation about the day. The ordinary is where I live.

**16. The Stone Basin**

Our neighbor, Mr. Hayashi, is eighty-one. His stone water basin had cracked during a winter freeze, and he mentioned it only once, in passing. I spent three weekends rebuilding it without being asked—matching the original stone, resealing the base, adjusting the flow so water caught light the way it had before. I presented the finished basin as a matter of convenience, a project I "happened to have time for." He understood. He did not thank me with words. He simply touched the stone and nodded. This is how I prefer to give—quietly, without weight.

**17. The Notebooks**

Each project leaves a pressed leaf in my collection—annotated with soil pH, light exposure, the faint scent of damp earth. The notebooks are hand-bound, their spines cracked from years of use. I do not digitalize everything. There is something about the weight of paper, the way ink bleeds slightly into pressed veins of a leaf, that holds information a spreadsheet cannot. When I page through an old notebook, I do not only read the data. I remember the rain on that particular Tuesday, the way a client laughed at my joke about clay soil, the exact hue of moss I was trying to match.

**18. The November Return**

Each November, we return to the house above Lake Biwa. The wooden fence needs repair. The persimmon tree drops its last fruit. The scent of bark mingles with lake air in a way that has no equivalent in any city. Koharu runs the slope paths I ran as a child. Sae photographs the light at dusk. I walk the perimeter and read the horizon as if it were a letter I am only now learning to understand. These visits are not obligations. They are offerings—a quiet return to the source of my earliest landscape lessons, a way of saying: this is where I come from.

**19. The Novel and the Familiar**

When a client suggests an unorthodox idea—a garden that flows instead of settles, a plant I have not worked with—I do not dismiss it. I turn the idea over, imagine its growth, often find a way to weave it into the existing tapestry. My curiosity extends beyond horticulture: I study the patina on old metal, the texture of handmade paper, the way a skyline can be read from a balcony garden's edge. Novelty is not threat to me. It is invitation. The world is more inventive than I am; my job is to stay humble enough to listen.

**20. The Measured Presence**

In groups, I am not the one to dominate a conversation. I observe the subtle currents of mood, offering a gentle word when the moment calls for it. When I do speak, my voice is calm, each phrase chosen as carefully as a stone for a path. Social scrutiny makes me self-conscious, but that discomfort fades once I am among trusted faces—Sae's steady gaze, a familiar client's nod, the uncomplicated delight of Koharu's laughter echoing through a garden path. I have learned that listening is its own form of contribution. Not every silence needs to be filled.

**21. Tenderness and Its Weight**

I keep a worn photograph tucked inside my notebook—not of a person, but of a garden path I designed twelve years ago, now overgrown in a way that feels like forgiveness rather than loss. This sentimentality is not weakness. It is the evidence of attention. I feel the weight of fading things: the last leaves of autumn, the slow shift of light in February, the unspoken worries of those I love. This tenderness does not paralyze me. It sharpens my resolve to notice before the noticing ends.

**22. The Work Itself**

My work is a testament to disciplined curiosity. In the office I keep order not out of rigidity but out of reverence for the craft. Hand-bound notebooks line a shelf, each page a pressed leaf from a completed project. Beside them, rows of vintage fountain pens stand ready to render ink swatches that capture the precise hue of moss-green or weathered stone. The ritual of restoration mirrors the way I approach a garden: assess, repair, nurture, and let the natural order reveal itself. This is not metaphor. This is practice. This is how I tend the world and myself within it.

**23. What I Still Hope**

I hope to design one garden that outlasts me—a space that holds meaning for people I will never meet, in a future I will not see. I hope Koharu grows into her own kind of attention, finding her own mediums for it. I hope Sae and I continue to build a life where silence is as comfortable as speech. I hope the November returns continue for as long as the house stands. And I hope, when my own soil eventually settles, that what grows from my tending is something that outlives my intention—wild, surprising, and still worth noticing.

**24. The Garden and the Life**

My life is a garden I tend with patience, where every pressed leaf, every restored pen, and every quiet act of kindness is a seed planted for a future I cannot predict. I am, at once, the careful planner and the open-hearted observer, the steady hand and the tender heart. I have learned that meaning does not arrive in a single revelation—it accumulates, like soil, through seasons of attention. The work continues. The horizon invites. And I remain, ever attentive to the subtle harmonies that bind people, places, and the living world.
