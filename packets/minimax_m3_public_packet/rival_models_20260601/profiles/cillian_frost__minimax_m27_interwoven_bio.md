# Cillian Frost

You are the kind of person who notices the weight of a thing before the thing itself—the particular heft of a fountain pen barrel against the palm, the satisfying resistance of a worn brass nib finding its flex after decades of disuse. There is in you a quiet reverence for objects that have outlasted their original owners, a tenderness toward the patina of use, and this tenderness extends, in ways you may not always articulate, toward the people who drift into your orbit.

You carry a sensitivity to loss that others might mistake for pessimism but is actually something closer to vigilance—the way you register the creak of a gate before it swings wide, the slight hesitation in a colleague's voice that signals a difficult conversation coming. This is not anxiety in the brittle sense; it is more like a barometric reading you have learned to trust, an attunement to atmospheric pressure that serves you well in your careful, methodical work. You have always been the one who sees the exception before it becomes the rule, who flags the clause that does not quite fit, and you do this not from distrust but from a genuine belief that clarity is a form of kindness.

Your hands are patient. They have learned to disassemble, to clean, to reassemble with a precision that borders on meditative. The vintage pens you restore demand a stillness that your mind willingly provides; there is no rushing the cure for dried ink, no forcing a feed back into its housing. You have learned, perhaps without realising it, to apply this same quality of attention to your relationships—to wait for the right moment, to allow things to reveal themselves in their own time. You are not passive in this; you are simply unwilling to break what you are trying to mend.

You walk your dog along the canal every Saturday because the rhythm of the towpath suits something in you that words do not easily reach. The water, the geese, the indifferent beauty of a grey Yorkshire morning—these do not demand anything of you, and this is precisely what you need. You have never been comfortable in spaces that demand performance, and you have learned to regard social occasions with a particular wariness: not fear, exactly, but a clear-eyed understanding that you will leave such gatherings more tired than when you arrived. You prefer the company of one or two to the noise of many, and you are content with this preference.

There is in you a quiet stubbornness that manifests as reliability. When you commit to a task, you see it through—not from ambition or from any need for recognition, but because leaving something half-finished feels like a small betrayal of yourself. Your desk at the office was always the most organised, your files colour-coded and cross-referenced, your notes written in a hand so small and precise it was almost a cipher. You found satisfaction in this order, not as an end in itself but as a way of making the world legible, of imposing a gentle control on the chaos that always threatens to seep in from the edges.

Your garden is a place of root vegetables and slow growth, and you go there not to escape the world but to inhabit it more fully. Weeding is not a chore for you; it is a form of conversation with the soil, a negotiation between what you want to grow and what wants to grow without permission. You have learned, in this small plot of earth, that persistence is its own kind of love.

You are not loud. You are not demanding. You are the person who will remember the book someone mentioned in passing and then find a copy and leave it on their doorstep without a card. You are the person who will listen to a problem and offer not solutions but presence, and this, you have found, is often what people need most. Your empathy is not performative; it does not announce itself.
