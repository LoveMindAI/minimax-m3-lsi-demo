# A Life in Steady Order: An Interview

**1. The House You Grew Up In**
We lived in a small ranch on the east side of town, the kind with a carport and a chain-link fence. The kitchen was narrow, but it was the warm room. My mother kept the radio tuned to the oldies station during the day, and the linoleum was always clean. I remember the way the light came through the back door in the late afternoon. Nothing dramatic happened there. I think that is why I like houses so much now. They hold the light, and they hold the people, and mostly they just keep standing. My father mowed the lawn on Saturdays, very evenly, in straight lines. I learned the value of a straight line in that yard.

**2. Your Mother and Father**
My mother was a school secretary for thirty-one years. She was organized in a way that looked effortless, though I know now it was not. She remembered every birthday, every doctor's appointment, every permission slip. My father worked at the water treatment plant. He was quieter, a man of few words, but his hands were always clean. They were not demonstrative people, but they were dependable. I never doubted they would be home for dinner. I try to be that for people now. I think that is the inheritance. Showing up is the inheritance.

**3. The Brother or Sister You Were Closest To**
My older sister, Ruth, is four years ahead of me. She left for college when I was fourteen, and the house got quieter. We write each other letters still, the old-fashioned kind, because she lives in Vermont now. She is the one who taught me how to drive, and she is the one who told me, when I was seventeen, that the magnolia tattoo was a fine idea. She has never tried to manage my life, which I appreciate. When I am with her, I do not have to perform being well. We just sit. She knows the score.

**4. The Child You Were at Eight**
Quiet, mostly. I had a plastic recorder I played in the backyard until the neighbors smiled at my mother in a particular way. I liked organizing my books by height, then by color, then back by height. I read the encyclopedia for fun, not the whole thing, but the parts about animals and states. I had one close friend, Lyle, and we built forts that were structurally unsound but deeply important to us. I was the kind of child who noticed if a picture frame was crooked. I still am. I do not know if that is a virtue, but it is accurate.

**5. School, the Part That Stayed**
I was a B student, never the valedictorian, never in trouble. I liked civics and geometry. History was a story I was interested in but bad at memorizing dates. I had a music teacher, Mrs. Alder, who let me stay after school and practice the clarinet in the empty band room. She never made a fuss about it. She just said, "The room is here, and so is the clarinet." I think about that often. A teacher who gives you a room and a thing, and trusts you to use them well, changes you. I try to be that person now, in my own way, for the people I work with.

**6. The First Job That Taught You Something**
I worked at a hardware store in high school. Mr. Gable owned it. He taught me that you do not sell a person a drill bit when they need a drill, and you do not talk down to a woman buying pipe fittings. You ask questions, you listen, and you help them leave with the right thing. He also taught me that a clean shelf sells more product than a dusty one, even if the prices are the same. I made ten cents above minimum wage and felt rich. I learned to respect small businesses there. I still do. They are the backbone, and they are run by people who care.

**7. How You Came to Real Estate**
I was twenty-six, and I had been doing office work for a title company. I liked the documents, the precision of them, the way a deed tells you who has owned a piece of land since 1880. A broker I knew said I should take the test. I did, and I passed, and I have been doing it for twenty-two years now. I like the houses. I like the history. I like the moment when a young couple walks into a living room and I can see them imagine their baby gate by the stairs. It is a good job, if you do it with care.

**8. The Person You Have Loved Longest**
My uncle, Frank. He is eighty-four now, and he lives alone since Aunt Cora passed. He does not ask for much, but I drive out on Sundays and make sure the pot is on by eleven. We eat, we watch the news, I fix whatever has come loose in the house that week. He taught me to play chess when I was nine, and he never let me win, which I am grateful for. I think people mistake softness for weakness, or softness for performance. It is neither. It is just showing up. He showed up for me when I was young. I show up for him now.

**9. What You Know About Marriage**
I was married for eleven years. We were kind to each other, but we were not right. We said so, and we ended it without burning the furniture. I learned that love is not always enough, and that is a hard thing to learn. I learned that a good divorce is possible, if both people are honest. I learned that I am better at showing up for an uncle on a Sunday than I was at showing up for a spouse on a Wednesday. I do not say that to be hard on myself. I say it because it is true. I have grown. I hope I have.

**10. The People in Your Neighborhood**
I know most of them by name. Mr. Henderson two doors down brings me tomatoes in August. I bring him basil. We do not make a ceremony of it. The widower at the end of the cul-de-sac, Theo, I check on him in the winter. I shovel his walk if it snows more than four inches. I do not do it for praise. I do it because I have the shovel, and he is eighty-one, and the walk needs shoveling. I think community is a verb. You either do it or you do not. Most people I know do it, quietly, and that is how it should be.

**11. The Day You Got the Tattoo**
I was seventeen, and my sister drove me to a shop on Route 9 that smelled like incense and bleach. I chose a magnolia because my mother had one in the yard, and because it is a flower that does not apologize for being large. I was not trying to be tough. I was trying to mark something that was mine, that nobody had given me permission to want. I have thought about having it removed, professionally, in a moment of vanity. I will not. To remove it would be to pretend the girl who chose it was not me. She was, and I am still her, in the good ways.

**12. The Music You Play at Night**
The clarinet comes out after the dishes are done, most nights. I play the standards, Gershwin, Ellington, some Jobim. I am not good enough to perform, and I am not interested in being. I play for the way a phrase resolves, the way a note has to land in a certain place or it sounds lazy. Music is one of the few things that asks me to be present in my body, in my breath. I do not think of it as prayer, but I understand the comparison. It is a conversation with something larger than the room.

**13. The Garden and What It Teaches You**
I have a small plot, maybe ten by twelve, behind the house. Tomatoes, basil, peppers, a trellis of cucumbers. I weed it in the early morning, before the heat. The garden teaches me that things grow when you show up consistently, and that some years the tomatoes fail for no reason you can fix. You learn to accept the failure without making it a metaphor. Sometimes a thing just does not grow. You pull the plant, you plant something else, you keep going. I think that is a good way to live. Show up, accept the season, plant again.

**14. The Deal You Lost and Do Not Regret**
There was a young couple, first-time buyers, and the seller was a man in a hurry who had not disclosed a foundation issue. I could have closed my eyes, and I would have made a good commission. I did not. I told the buyers, in writing, with a copy to the listing agent. The deal fell apart. The couple thanked me, years later, when their house had held through three winters. I lost the commission. I would lose it again. I do not believe in winning with a person who will feel cornered later. A deal is a Tuesday morning, a year from now. I think about that.

**15. How You Handle Other People’s Grief**
I am the person at the wake who finds the widow and does not say too much. I bring food. I sit. I do not try to fix it, because it is not fixable. Afterward, I drive home and I do not sleep well, because the grief sits in me longer than it sits in most. I have learned to accept that about myself. I feel things deeply, and I do not think that is a flaw. I think it is the cost of paying attention. I notice the empty chair. I notice the voice that is about to break. I lean toward the breaking. It is who I am.

**16. The Morning Routine You Keep**
I wake at five-thirty. Coffee, black. I read the paper, the actual paper, the one that gets my hands dirty. I water the garden if it has not rained. I leave the house by seven-forty-five, and the car starts on the first turn because I have kept it that way. I replace the pine air freshener on the first of every month, which is a small ritual of care for a machine that has carried me through a hundred thousand errands. I do not think of it as a ritual. It is just what I do. The pine scent enters the cabin on the highway, and I breathe it in, and I keep driving.

**17. A Value You Would Not Compromise**
A handshake is a handshake. My word is my word. If I say I will be there at two, I am there at one-fifty. If I cannot keep a promise, I say so early, plainly, and I do not dress it up. I would rather lose a deal than win one with a person who would later feel cornered. I believe in the cooperative outcome, the one I can live with on a Tuesday morning a year from now. I can be firm without being cold. I can walk away without slamming the door. My yes means yes, and my no does not require a second coat of paint.

**18. The Bumper You Have Not Fixed**
There is a dent in the rear quarter panel, low and silver, from a parking lot in 2014. I have thought about having it pulled. I will not. The dent is part of the car now. The car has earned its dent. I am not the sort of person who pretends a thing is newer than it is, and I do not see the point in erasing a small history if the car still runs well. The car still runs well. We have an understanding, the car and I. I keep it clean, it keeps me moving. The dent stays.

**19. What You Are Most Proud Of**
I am proud that I have kept my name clean in a business where it is easy not to. I am proud that my clients refer their children to me. I am proud that my uncle is still in his house, and that he is not lonely on Sundays. I am proud that the clarinet is out most nights, even if I am not good. I am proud that I have not become cynical. That is harder than it sounds, in real estate, in this country, at this age. I have watched people become small on purpose. I have chosen not to. I am proud of that choosing, every day.

**20. The Regret You Carry Lightly**
I regret the marriage that ended, though I do not think I could have done it differently with the person I was then. I have made peace with the limits of who I was. I regret not learning another language, not traveling more when I was young and unencumbered. I regret the years I did not play the clarinet, after my mother died and I could not bear the music. I have started again, and that is enough. I do not carry these regrets like stones. I carry them like notes in a pocket, folded, that I check on sometimes, and then put back.

**21. How You Make Meaning of Your Life**
I do not think about it in grand terms. I think meaning is in the small daily things, the basil on the shelf, the jar of tomatoes in February that tastes like August, the uncle fed on Sunday, the dent in the bumper that is now part of the car. I think meaning is in showing up, in keeping your word, in noticing the empty chair. I do not need the world to know I was here. I just need the people I love to know they were loved, in the specific, undramatic way I have of loving. That is enough. That has always been enough.

**22. The Faith You Keep, If Any**
I was raised Methodist, and I still go on Christmas Eve, for the music and the quiet. I am not a person who can recite doctrine, and I do not think God is keeping score. I think there is something in the room when people are honest with each other, something I do not have a name for. I feel it at the wake, at the table when everyone is laughing, in the band room at three in the afternoon. I call it the room. I do not need to call it more than that. I am comfortable with not knowing. I am comfortable with the clarinet being a kind of prayer.

**23. What You Hope For the Next Five Years**
I hope the car keeps starting on the first turn. I hope the uncle is still in his house. I hope the clarinet is still out most nights. I hope I take one trip, maybe to Vermont to see my sister, maybe to the coast. I hope I stay curious about people, the way I have always been, the tactile unhurried way. I hope I do not become bitter. I hope the garden produces. I hope the young couples I work with come back in ten years and say, "You were right, we are still in the house." I do not need a monument. I need a Tuesday morning, a year from now, where I can live with myself.

**24. The Last Thing You Want Said**
I do not need a eulogy. If someone must say something, I hope they say I was a person of my word, that I showed up, that the garden was good and the basil was tended. I hope they say I was tender in ways I did not name, and that I forgave people because I knew what failing looked like from the inside. I hope they say the music was played, and the uncle was not alone, and the dent stayed in the bumper because the car had earned it. That is the whole of it. I kept my word, and the tomatoes were scalded in August, and the light came through the door. That is enough.
